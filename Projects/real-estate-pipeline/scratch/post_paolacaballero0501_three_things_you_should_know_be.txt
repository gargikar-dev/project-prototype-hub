# Before Hiring an Outbound Agency: 3 Key Considerations

Three things you should know before hiring an agency for outbound (before having to learn this the hard way) 1. Do they own your infrastructure or do you? You should own it. If they disappear or you end the contract, you don't want to start over from scratch. 2. How do they communicate when something isn't working? The good agencies tell you first. The bad ones wait for you to ask. 3. What does offboarding look like? If they can't explain what you walk away with, data, domains, lead lists, campaign history, that's a red flag. The best agency relationships feel like a real partnership. You're in the loop, you understand what's happening, and at the end of the engagement you're better off than when you started. That's the bar. It's not that high. But it does filter out a lot. Anything you'd add to this list?🤔

"I don't think cold email works anymore." A recruitment agency told us that during our first call. They had tried multiple campaigns over the previous few months. Some emails were beautifully written. Some were heavily personalized. A few even had impressive open rates. But very few conversations turned into real hiring opportunities. Their conclusion was simple: "Cold email has stopped working." Instead of rewriting another email, we asked them to walk us through their entire outreach process. That changed the conversation. The biggest issue wasn't the copy. It was that every campaign was being treated like a one-time event instead of a repeatable system. Together, they started measuring conversations instead of opens, refined who they were reaching, stayed consistent with follow-ups, and reviewed results after every campaign. Within weeks, the quality of replies began to improve and so did the number of qualified conversations. One lesson from that project has stayed with me: The best outreach systems aren't built on perfect emails. They're built on consistent learning. If you run outbound for your business, what's one change that made the biggest difference: better messaging, better targeting, or better process? One line kills more cold emails than bad copy ever will: the personalized opener that isn't actually personalized. Most reps think personalization means the company name or a compliment on the website. That's just a mail-merge with extra steps. Real personalization means referencing an actual event: they just raised funding, made a hire, posted a role, published something. That single reference does more work than the rest of the email combined. Everything after barely matters if the signal is right. One sharp question, proof you understand their situation. One sentence of credibility, a number, never your company history. One ask that costs them nothing, an insight or an intro, never a meeting request. Most GTM teams spend 80% of their time polishing the message and 20% finding the right trigger. Flip that ratio and reply rate takes care of itself. A recruiter opening with "saw you just posted three openings for account executives" gets replies a generic "hope you're doing well" never will. Signal quality beats clever copy every time.

10.8% is our average reply rate when advertising sales candidates at [Scout](https://www.linkedin.com/company/tryscoutai?trk=public_post-text). My thoughts on why these campaigns crush: 1. Hiring salespeople is a growth signal. If a company is adding sellers, they're doing well and have budget to spend. 2. Great salespeople are rare and passive. There are a LOT of sellers out there, but the great ones are usually happy where they are. So when you tell a hiring manager you have a candidate who crushed it in their exact industry, with the numbers to prove it, and they're not actively looking, that beats anything in their inbound applicant pool. 3. Sales leaders actually reply. They live in their inbox and on LinkedIn, and they're far more open to a conversation than a technical hiring manager. If you're a recruiter with strong sales talent on your bench, market them aggressively. It's the fastest path we've seen to landing new clients. Curious if any recruiters out there are having this much success advertising other types of candidates?

Referrals are not a growth strategy. I know that stings but someone needs to say it. Referrals are great. They close fast. They trust you from day one. But you can't control when they come in. You can't scale them. And when they dry up you're sat there refreshing your inbox waiting for the phone to ring. I've spoken to hundreds of agency owners. The ones stuck at the same revenue for 2, 3, 4 years? Same story every time. "We get most of our work from referrals." That's not a strategy. That's luck with a nice name. The agencies actually growing right now aren't waiting to be found. They're putting themselves in front of decision-makers every single week. Not cold calling. Not spamming LinkedIn. Not hiring a BD rep for £5K/month who quits after 90 days. They're using systems that do the outreach for them so they can focus on what they're good at. Recruiting. Referrals should be the cherry on top. Not the whole cake. The graveyard of outbound isn't filled with bad emails. It's filled with emails that arrived at the wrong moment. I've seen companies spend weeks rewriting outbound campaigns. They test new subject lines, add more personalization, and refine every sentence. Sometimes reply rates improve. More often, they don't. Not because the email was bad. Because the prospect had no reason to care that week. Timing is one of the most overlooked parts of outbound. A new executive joins. A company raises capital. A strategic initiative is announced. A hiring push begins. Those moments change how a message is received. The exact same email that gets ignored today can start a conversation next month because something inside the business has changed. That's why we spend less time asking, "How can we write a better email?" And more time asking, "Why would this company care right now?" The best outbound isn't built on perfect copy. It's built on relevance. And relevance starts with timing.

Most recruitment agencies measure BD by the wrong things. Calls made. Emails sent. LinkedIn connections accepted. Meetings booked. These are activity metrics. They tell you whether people are busy. "We're looking for someone to send cold emails." Weakest job description in outbound. And people post it every week. Here's the thing nobody says out loud. Cold email was never one job. It's six. Stacked in a trench coat, pretending to be one hire. You post the JD. One person shows up. Now they're quietly drowning in five roles and half-doing the sixth. Then you wonder why replies went flat. So instead of hiring one person to be a whole team, set up one system for the team you already have. Six roles, one system. [Claude](https://www.linkedin.com/showcase/claude/?trk=public_post-text) and [Saleshandy](https://in.linkedin.com/company/saleshandy?trk=public_post-text) doing the heavy lifting underneath. Here's what it actually covers: → Lead Sourcer: Describe your ICP in plain English, get a list back from 800M+ contacts. Plus buying signals, so you hit the ones already ready. → Data Researcher: Enriches missing emails and strips bounces before you send. Not after. → Copywriter: Turns your ICP into a full sequence with follow-ups that add a reason to reply. Not "just circling back." → Deliverability Eng: Warm-up, inbox audits, sender rotation, SPF/DKIM/DMARC. The boring stuff that quietly decides everything. → Outreach Rep: Email, dialer, LinkedIn, WhatsApp. One flow, not four tabs. → RevOps: Every reply in one inbox, auto-tagged by intent, deals on a Kanban board. Now the hard pill. Hiring more won't fix this. When replies drop, your instinct is to add a person. But you don't need six people. You need one system that already does all six. That's the whole thing.
